    Hydro-Jetting for Grease Lines in Broken Arrow, OK

    A cable machine pokes a hole through grease. Jetting actually removes it. Our jetters run around 3,000 to 4,000 psi through a spinning nozzle, which scours the whole inside of the pipe and pushes the loosened grease downstream. A camera pass afterward shows whether the line is really clean.

    Everything in the trap comes out, from the floating grease cap down to the sludge of food solids on the floor. The industry calls it brown grease. It is different from the yellow grease in your used fryer oil bin, which is recycled separately and should never be poured into the trap.

    It is the most common pumping standard. Pump before grease and solids fill a quarter of the depth. Past that point the water moves through too fast for grease to separate, so grease rides out the outlet even though the trap looks like it is working.

    Yes, both. Both show up a lot in commercial kitchens, Thermaco mostly as Big Dipper AGRUs. We clean the skimmer wheels, strainer baskets and heater areas on automatic units, and we stock the gaskets and flow control fittings that fail most often on the passive ones.

    Under-sink hydromechanical traps and in-ground gravity interceptors make up most of our work. Automatic grease removal units are on the list too. Those AGRUs have a heater and a skimmer wheel that pull grease into a container, and they need the container emptied and the wheel and screen cleaned on a schedule.

    It depends on trap size and menu. Most restaurants end up on a two-week to three-month interval. A home with a small under-sink unit might only need it once a year, while a busy fryer kitchen with a 50-gallon trap can need it every other week.

    The terms get mixed up constantly. Generally a trap is the small unit indoors, often under 100 gallons. An interceptor is the big in-ground tank outside, often 500 gallons or more, and plumbing codes treat them as separate categories with different sizing rules.
