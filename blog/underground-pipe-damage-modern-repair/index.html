    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. They stay out of mind until the lawn gets soggy or a toilet backs up. A typical lateral is 4-inch pipe, and older homes often have clay tile or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. Once a hair-thin root gets in, it grows into a mat that catches paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. Its joints come every couple of feet, and a big maple or oak can pack a 4-inch line full in a few growing seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Most homes sit on concrete slabs, so leaks under the floor often call for professional leak detection before repair.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. Rigid pipe like clay or cast iron does not bend, so it either cracks or separates at a joint. Sags are common too. A belly in the line holds standing water and waste, and the camera shows it as a section that stays full after a flush.

    Old Pipe Material

    Much of the housing in Oklahoma dates from the mid-century boom onward, with many older homes on slabs with cast iron drains underneath.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg, which builders used into the early 1970s, is tar-soaked fiber pipe that slowly squashes flat. Old galvanized water lines rust shut inside and drop the pressure everywhere.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. A gurgling toilet when the washing machine drains is a classic sign. In the yard, watch for a strip of grass that is greener than the rest, or ground that feels soft over the line during dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. With everything in the house shut off, watch the small leak triangle or dial on the meter. A spinning indicator means water is leaving somewhere between the meter and the house, and a buried service line is a likely spot. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. We run a camera through the cleanout and record the whole line, with a locator that marks depth and position on the ground above. You see the same video we do. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. A collapsed section or a line with a deep belly usually has to be dug up, since lining needs a pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. A single broken joint under the yard might mean a 4 by 6 foot hole and a day of work.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. We pull a resin-soaked felt liner into the old pipe, inflate it, and let it cure into a new pipe inside the old one. Most residential liners go in through the cleanout and one small access pit, and the job takes a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A cone-shaped head is winched through the old pipe, splitting it and pulling new HDPE pipe into place behind it. You need a pit on each end, but the lawn in between stays untouched. Our crews at JBK Plumbing use both methods across Oklahoma, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Each backup leaves waste in the line, and roots grow faster with that steady food supply. A typical camera inspection takes less than an hour, and we hand you the video along with the marked location of the damage. Reach JBK Plumbing at (405) 507-8396. Ask about the cleanout location when you call, since an accessible cleanout saves time.
